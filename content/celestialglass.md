CelestialGlass is a watch face for navigators. The edge of the watch is the horizon, marked as a compass. The sky is a clock that turns through the day. The weather, a tropical storm and the tide are each one press away. The pictures are set off Portsmouth, in the approaches to the Solent, on a November evening in the twilight when star sights are taken.

**To print and read before you sail:** [the guide](/docs/celestialglass/CelestialGlass_user_guide.pdf) (PDF): every screen drawn large, with a line to each part.

## The face

1. **The rim is the horizon.** North is always at the top. The best three or four stars and planets for the next twilight fix are named round it at the bearings they will have then.
2. **The sky is a clock.** A 24-hour ring turns so that now is at the top, coloured for day, the three twilights and night. An arc for each body shows when it can be shot.
3. **The centre** holds the time in Nixie tubes, the barometer and its forecast, and when the fix twilight opens or closes.
4. **At night** it turns red to keep your night vision.

## Four screens, one press apart

Press and hold the screen for about a second to step from one to the next.

{{< figure src="/images/celestialglass/sight.png" alt="Sight mode: the fix stars named large at their bearings, and the time in Zulu" class="watch small" >}}

1. **Sight:** for the ten minutes that matter. The fix stars in large type at their bearings, and the time in Zulu for the sight book. Everything else is gone.
{{< figure src="/images/celestialglass/cyclones.png" alt="Tropical Cyclones mode: the storm's centre bearing, Bowditch's band in deep red with the margin either side, DANGEROUS SIDE?" class="watch small" >}}

2. **Tropical Cyclones:** Buys Ballot's law as a picture. From the wind and the barometer, where a storm's centre lies, which side of it you are on, and what the old rule says to do. *A teaching aid, not for use at sea.*
{{< figure src="/images/celestialglass/tide.png" alt="Tide mode: a ring of water with now at the top, high water at 18:31 and low water at 00:43" class="watch small" >}}

3. **Tide:** twelve hours of tide as a ring of water, with now at the top, the turns and their slack water, and the sun and moon rising and setting.

## The storm, in plain words

The Tropical Cyclones screen says what the navigator's old rule says, from Bowditch, *The American Practical Navigator*, chapter 39:

1. Look the way the storm is going: on its right the wind veers, on its left it backs.
2. North of the equator its right is the dangerous side; south of it, its left.
3. A steady shift says *probably*. A boat under way can see the opposite shift from her own motion; if in doubt, stop and watch.
4. Face the wind: north of the equator the centre is about 110 degrees to your right ahead of the storm, and 120 to 135 behind it; south of the equator, to your left. Nearer the centre, a point or two less.
5. The navigable side is not safe, only less dangerous.
6. On the wrist: DANGEROUS SIDE? or NAVIGABLE SIDE?, with a small triangle marked 3h where the wind was. The question mark always stays. A second swing that agrees is shown another way.

On Bowditch's full sign for the storm's path (the wind steady, its speed rising, the glass falling) it says IN OR NEAR ITS PATH, then STOP AND WATCH and GET THE ADVISORY, and shows no course. A steady wind that is not rising shows SIDE NOT CLEAR: STOP AND WATCH.

### Why it shows no course near a storm's path

{{< figure src="/images/celestialglass/w1-courses.png" alt="A boat 93 miles to the right of a storm's track: running with the wind on the quarter takes her to 58 miles from the centre; stopping, 94; Bowditch's dangerous-side rule, 205" >}}

Far from a storm, a boat on its track and a boat many miles to one side both see a steady wind that is slowly rising, and the glass falling. Bowditch's course for a boat on the track ahead of the storm is to run with the wind on the quarter. For a boat off to the side, on the dangerous side, that course can be the worst one.

The picture is a boat stopped 93 miles to the right of a storm's track, 223 miles from the centre, the storm moving 330° at 12 knots. Over 24 hours at 8 knots:

1. **Running** with the wind on the starboard quarter (steering 265) brings her to **58 miles** from the centre, inside its gale-force winds.
2. **Stopping** and watching keeps her 94 miles off.
3. **Bowditch's rule for the side she is really on** (wind on the starboard bow, steering 020) keeps her **205 miles** off.

So the face gives the honest answer, "in or near the path", and sends you to the advisory: Bowditch's way to the right semicircle is to plot the storm's positions from the warnings.

It takes the wind from Garmin's weather on your phone. Offshore with no phone it says NO WIND REPORT.

## A fair warning

CelestialGlass tells you where to look and when. It is not for working a position: bearings are good to about a tenth of a degree and times to a minute or two. Its tide times show when the tide turns, not heights, and real tides shift with the weather and the coast.
